## Question 1

*People*

**A newly formed agile team asks why the project manager spent the first week with the sponsor and future users agreeing on a one-paragraph statement of the outcome the product must deliver. What is the main purpose of that statement?** Choose one.

- **a.** Fix the scope baseline before work can begin
- **b.** Replace the product backlog for the first release
- **c.** Guide trade-off decisions toward one shared outcome
- **d.** Capture each stakeholder's individual requirements in one place

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: c**

- **a** ❌ A one-paragraph outcome statement is not a scope baseline, and an agile team does not freeze scope up front. Scope stays emergent within the vision.
- **b** ❌ A vision does not list or order work items. The backlog still holds the work; the vision is what the product owner uses to order it.
- **c** ✅ A vision states why the work exists and what outcome counts as success, so the team and stakeholders can make consistent trade-offs when choices arise.
- **d** ❌ Collecting individual requirements produces a requirements list, not a single shared outcome. The point of the statement is that everyone agrees on one direction.

**The concept.** A common vision is a brief, shared statement of the intended outcome and value of the work. It is the reference point for decisions, not a plan or a scope document.

**Why this is correct.** The statement is short and outcome-focused, and it was agreed with the sponsor and users. That is what a vision is for: giving everyone the same answer to 'why are we doing this?' so trade-offs point the same way. It does not replace the backlog, freeze scope or capture individual requirements.

**How to reason it out**

1. Notice the statement is one paragraph about an outcome, not a list of work.
2. Notice it was agreed jointly by sponsor and users.
3. Match 'shared outcome that guides choices' to the purpose of a vision.
4. Rule out options that describe scope, requirements or backlog artifacts.

> **Exam tip:** The vision answers 'why' and guides decisions; the backlog and scope answer 'what'.

</details>

## Question 2

*People*

**At the start of a predictive construction project, the sponsor asks where the project's high-level purpose, measurable objectives and success criteria will be formally recorded and authorized. Which document should the project manager point to?** Choose one.

- **a.** Project charter
- **b.** Stakeholder register
- **c.** Requirements traceability matrix
- **d.** Project scope statement

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: a**

- **a** ✅ The charter formally authorizes the project and records its purpose, measurable objectives and success criteria. It is the predictive home of the high-level vision.
- **b** ❌ The stakeholder register lists who is involved and their interests. It does not record the project's purpose or success criteria.
- **c** ❌ The traceability matrix links requirements to deliverables and objectives. It references the objectives but is not where they are authorized.
- **d** ❌ The scope statement describes deliverables, exclusions and acceptance criteria in detail. It comes after the charter and does not authorize the project.

**The concept.** In predictive projects the project charter is the document that authorizes the project and captures its purpose, objectives and success criteria: the formal expression of the project vision.

**Why this is correct.** The sponsor asks for three things together (purpose, measurable objectives, success criteria) plus formal authorization. Only the charter does all of these. The scope statement is the tempting choice because it is also an early document, but it details deliverables and does not authorize the project.

**How to reason it out**

1. Identify what must be recorded: purpose, objectives, success criteria.
2. Identify the extra requirement: formal authorization.
3. Recall that the charter authorizes the project and holds its high-level purpose.
4. Rule out documents that elaborate scope, people or traceability.

> **Exam tip:** Predictive projects anchor their vision in the charter: purpose, objectives, success criteria and authorization.

</details>

## Question 3

*People*

**An agile team wants a lightweight artifact that says, in a few sentences, who the product is for, which need it meets and how it differs from alternatives, so anyone on the team can repeat it. Which artifact fits best?** Choose one.

- **a.** Team definition of done
- **b.** Iteration goal statement
- **c.** Product vision statement
- **d.** Release-level product roadmap

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: c**

- **a** ❌ The definition of done sets the quality criteria every increment must meet. It says nothing about who the product serves or why.
- **b** ❌ An iteration goal states what one iteration aims to achieve. It is short-lived and narrower than a statement about the whole product.
- **c** ✅ A product vision statement (often written as an elevator statement) names the target customer, the need and the differentiator in a few memorable sentences.
- **d** ❌ A product roadmap shows how the product is expected to evolve across releases over time. It sequences delivery; it is not a few sentences naming customer, need and differentiator.

**The concept.** A product vision statement captures target customer, need and differentiation in a short form the whole team can remember and repeat.

**Why this is correct.** The stem asks for a few sentences covering customer, need and differentiation that anyone can repeat. That is the classic product vision (elevator) statement. The iteration goal is tempting because it is also short, but it covers one iteration, not the product.

**How to reason it out**

1. List what the artifact must contain: customer, need, differentiator.
2. Note it must be short and stable enough to repeat.
3. Match it to the product vision statement.
4. Rule out artifacts about quality, one iteration or the work list.

> **Exam tip:** Customer, need and differentiator in a few sentences = product vision statement.

</details>

## Question 4

*People*

**On a predictive construction project, the site engineer and the facade subcontractor argue in every coordination meeting. The project manager reviews the resource calendar and finds both crews booked for the only tower crane during the same two weeks. What is the main source of this conflict?** Choose one.

- **a.** Incompatible personal working styles
- **b.** Disagreement over the approved cost baseline
- **c.** Unclear technical requirements for the facade
- **d.** Competing demand for a scarce resource

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: d**

- **a** ❌ The arguments happen in meetings, but the calendar shows a concrete double booking. Treating it as a personality clash would leave the real cause untouched.
- **b** ❌ Cost is not mentioned. The crews are competing for time on one crane, which is a resource and scheduling issue rather than a budget dispute.
- **c** ❌ Nothing in the scenario suggests the facade specification is disputed; both crews know what they need to do, they just need the same equipment to do it.
- **d** ✅ Both crews need the single crane in the same window. Scarce, shared resources are one of the most common sources of project conflict, and the calendar evidence points straight at it.

**The concept.** Identifying the source of a conflict comes before choosing how to resolve it.

**Why this is correct.** The resource calendar shows two crews booked on the one crane for the same weeks, so the conflict comes from competing demand for a scarce resource. Personality and requirements explanations are tempting because the symptom is arguing in meetings, but the evidence points at the double booking, and fixing that removes the trigger.

**How to reason it out**

1. Look past the symptom (arguing) to the evidence (the resource calendar).
2. Notice that two crews need one crane at the same time.
3. Name the source: competing demand for a scarce resource.
4. Choose a response that addresses that source, not the behaviour.

> **Exam tip:** Find the source first: a double-booked resource is a resource conflict, however personal the arguments sound.

</details>

## Question 5

*People*

**A new hybrid project team brings together staff from three departments. In the first week they disagree about core working hours, how fast messages should be answered and where decisions get recorded. The project manager wants these norms settled before delivery work begins. What should the project manager do?** Choose one.

- **a.** Publish ground rules drawn from company policy
- **b.** Ask the functional managers to set team norms
- **c.** Facilitate the team in drafting a team charter
- **d.** Record the norms in the communications plan

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: c**

- **a** ❌ Company policy can inform the rules, but rules handed down by the project manager are imposed rather than agreed. The disagreement is about how this particular team will work, which the team needs to settle together.
- **b** ❌ Functional managers do not work inside the team day to day, so norms they set would be external and less likely to be owned. It also pushes the project manager's team-building job onto line management.
- **c** ✅ A team charter (or working agreement) is created by the team itself and records its values, ground rules, working hours, response expectations and decision practices. Because the members write it, they own it and are more likely to follow it.
- **d** ❌ The communications management plan covers information flows to stakeholders, not the team's internal ground rules. Writing norms into it without the team agreeing them does not resolve the disagreement.

**The concept.** A team charter, also called a working agreement, is the team's own record of its values, ground rules and working norms.

**Why this is correct.** The deciding fact is that norms stick when the team creates them. Facilitating the team to draft a charter turns a disagreement into shared agreements. Publishing rules from policy is tempting because it is quick, but it is imposed rather than agreed; the communications plan is the wrong document; functional managers are outside the team.

**How to reason it out**

1. Notice the problem is about how this team will work together.
2. Recall that team-level expectations are set by the team, facilitated by the leader.
3. Pick the option where the team itself writes the agreement.
4. Reject options that impose norms or put them in a stakeholder document.

> **Exam tip:** Team norms belong in a team charter that the team writes itself.

</details>

## Question 6

*People*

**The sponsor has just signed the charter for a predictive project to relocate a regional warehouse. Planning has not started, and the project manager so far knows only the sponsor and two department heads. What should the project manager do next?** Choose one.

- **a.** Write the engagement plan around the three known people
- **b.** Draft the communications schedule for the known contacts
- **c.** Send the charter to the two department heads for sign-off
- **d.** Identify everyone the relocation affects and record them

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: d**

- **a** ❌ An engagement plan built on three people would miss most of the affected groups. Identification and analysis come before the plan that depends on them.
- **b** ❌ A schedule for known contacts is premature. Communication planning depends on knowing who the stakeholders are and what they need.
- **c** ❌ The charter is already signed by the sponsor, who authorizes it. Collecting extra sign-offs does not discover the stakeholders who are still unknown.
- **d** ✅ Stakeholder identification starts as soon as the charter exists and before detailed planning, so that planning reflects every group the relocation touches (drivers, customers, landlord, local authority and so on).

**The concept.** Stakeholder identification begins early, at or right after chartering, and feeds every later plan.

**Why this is correct.** The deciding detail is timing: the charter is signed and planning has not begun. PMI expects the project manager to identify stakeholders first, because the engagement and communication plans are built from that list. Writing either plan around three people locks in an incomplete picture.

**How to reason it out**

1. Note where the project is: charter signed, planning not started.
2. Recall that stakeholder identification is one of the first activities after chartering.
3. Recognise that engagement and communication plans depend on a complete stakeholder list.
4. Choose the action that widens the list before planning.

> **Exam tip:** Identify stakeholders first; plan how to engage and communicate with them second.

</details>

## Question 7

*People*

**A project manager's stakeholder analysis places the regional finance director in the high-power, low-interest quadrant. The director rarely reads project updates but can stop funding for the next phase. How should the project manager engage this stakeholder?** Choose one.

- **a.** Keep satisfied with concise, targeted updates
- **b.** Monitor with minimal communication effort
- **c.** Keep informed through the detailed weekly status report
- **d.** Manage closely through regular one-to-one working sessions

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: a**

- **a** ✅ High power with low interest calls for keeping the stakeholder satisfied: enough targeted information to stay comfortable, without burdening someone who is not following the detail.
- **b** ❌ Monitoring suits stakeholders with low power and low interest. Someone who can stop funding cannot be left with minimal attention.
- **c** ❌ Keeping informed suits low-power, high-interest stakeholders. A director who does not read updates will not be engaged by a routine report, even though they can stop the funding.
- **d** ❌ Managing closely suits stakeholders with both high power and high interest. This director is not interested in the detail, so frequent working sessions would waste their time and erode goodwill.

**The concept.** A power/interest grid categorizes stakeholders into four quadrants, each with a default engagement approach: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed and monitor.

**Why this is correct.** Power decides how much the stakeholder can affect the project; interest decides how much detail they want. High power with low interest means keep satisfied. The tempting near-twin is keep informed, which is the approach for the opposite quadrant (low power, high interest).

**How to reason it out**

1. Read the stakeholder's power: they can stop funding, so it is high.
2. Read their interest: they rarely read updates, so it is low.
3. Map high power and low interest to keep satisfied.
4. Reject the approaches that belong to the other three quadrants.

> **Exam tip:** High power, low interest: keep satisfied, not keep informed.

</details>

## Question 8

*Process*

**A project manager will lead an internal mobile app for field technicians. Supervisors and technicians disagree about which features matter, and the mobile platform is new to the developers. The sponsor wants a delivery approach recommendation next week. What should the project manager do first?** Choose one.

- **a.** Ask the sponsor which delivery approach the organization prefers
- **b.** Decompose the expected features into a detailed WBS
- **c.** Assess how uncertain the requirements and technology are
- **d.** Adopt the organization's standard Scrum framework

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: c**

- **a** ❌ The sponsor asked the project manager for a recommendation. Handing the choice back without analysis gives the sponsor nothing to decide on.
- **b** ❌ A detailed WBS assumes the features are known and agreed. Here they are disputed, and choosing to decompose them presumes a predictive approach before it has been assessed.
- **c** ✅ The disagreement about features and the unfamiliar platform are the two inputs that decide the approach. Assessing them first gives the recommendation a basis.
- **d** ❌ Picking a framework by default skips the assessment. The approach should be tailored to the project's needs, not chosen out of habit.

**The concept.** Before recommending a development approach, the project manager assesses the project's needs, complexity and magnitude, especially how uncertain the requirements and the technology are.

**Why this is correct.** Two facts in the stem matter: stakeholders disagree about features (requirements uncertainty) and the platform is new (technical uncertainty). PMI expects the project manager to understand the situation before acting, so assessing these factors comes first. A WBS presumes stable scope, a default framework skips tailoring, and passing the choice back ignores the request for a recommendation.

**How to reason it out**

1. Spot the signals: disputed features and an unfamiliar platform.
2. Recognise both as uncertainty factors that drive the choice of approach.
3. Apply 'assess before acting': analyse before recommending.
4. Reject options that presume an approach or hand the decision away.

> **Exam tip:** Assess requirements and technical uncertainty first; the approach recommendation follows from it.

</details>

## Question 9

*Process*

**A PMO is reviewing two proposals. Project A will replace 2,000 identical branch printers using a fixed, proven configuration across 300 sites. Project B is a five-person pilot of a new AI triage tool whose users cannot yet describe what they need. Which delivery approach recommendation best fits the two projects?** Choose one.

- **a.** A: detailed upfront plan; B: detailed upfront plan with a larger contingency reserve
- **b.** A: short iterations by site wave; B: detailed upfront plan and baseline
- **c.** A: short iterations by site wave; B: short iterations with user feedback
- **d.** A: detailed upfront plan and baseline; B: short iterations with user feedback

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: d**

- **a** ❌ Extra contingency covers known risks with uncertain outcomes; it does not help when users cannot yet state what they need. B's requirements have to be discovered, not baselined up front.
- **b** ❌ This treats size as if it were complexity. A's large scale does not make it uncertain, and B's small team does not make its requirements stable enough to baseline.
- **c** ❌ B suits iterations, but a repeatable rollout of a fixed, proven configuration gains little from iterating; its uncertainty is low, so a predictive plan is more efficient.
- **d** ✅ A is large in magnitude but well understood, so a detailed predictive plan fits. B is small but its requirements are unknown, which is where short adaptive iterations with users pay off.

**The concept.** Magnitude (size, number of sites, budget) and complexity (uncertainty in requirements and technology) are separate assessments. The approach follows uncertainty, not size.

**Why this is correct.** Project A is big but routine: a fixed, proven configuration repeated many times, which suits a detailed predictive plan. Project B is small but its users cannot describe their needs, the classic case for short adaptive iterations. Reversing the two is the trap for anyone who equates 'large' with 'complex'; adding contingency reserve to B treats unknown requirements as a risk to be funded rather than discovered; iterating on A spends adaptive overhead on work with little uncertainty.

**How to reason it out**

1. Rate each project's magnitude: A large, B small.
2. Rate each project's uncertainty: A low, B high.
3. Recall that the approach follows uncertainty, not size.
4. Choose predictive for the low-uncertainty project and adaptive for the high-uncertainty one.

> **Exam tip:** Big is not the same as complex: choose the approach from uncertainty, then scale the plan to the magnitude.

</details>

## Question 10

*Process*

**An engineering firm is planning the repair of a highway bridge. The design is complete and approved, permits fix the work sequence, and changes after concrete is poured would be very costly. Which development approach should the project manager recommend?** Choose one.

- **a.** Predictive
- **b.** Adaptive
- **c.** Incremental
- **d.** Iterative

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: a**

- **a** ✅ Stable, approved requirements, a fixed sequence and a high cost of late change are the conditions where planning the work up front pays off.
- **b** ❌ Adaptive approaches suit requirements that are expected to change. Here the design is approved and late change is very costly.
- **c** ❌ Incremental life cycles deliver usable pieces in stages. A bridge repair produces its value when the whole repair is complete.
- **d** ❌ Iterative life cycles refine a solution through repeated versions, which assumes the requirements are still being discovered.

**The concept.** A predictive life cycle fits when requirements are well understood and stable and the cost of change rises sharply once work is done.

**Why this is correct.** Every factor in the stem points the same way: the design is fixed, permits set the sequence and rework would be very costly. Those are the conditions in which a predictive plan with baselines is the best fit. Adaptive and iterative approaches assume evolving requirements; incremental delivery assumes the result is useful in pieces.

**How to reason it out**

1. Check requirement stability: the design is approved.
2. Check the cost of change: very high after pouring.
3. Check delivery: the value arrives when the repair is complete.
4. Match stable requirements and single delivery to predictive.

> **Exam tip:** Stable requirements plus a high cost of change point to a predictive approach.

</details>

## Question 11

*Process*

**During planning for a predictive office relocation, the sponsor wants a single document that describes the deliverables, how each will be accepted and what the project will not deliver. Which document should the project manager prepare?** Choose one.

- **a.** Project charter
- **b.** Requirements documentation
- **c.** Scope management plan
- **d.** Project scope statement

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: d**

- **a** ❌ The charter authorizes the project and gives high-level objectives and requirements. It is not detailed enough to define acceptance criteria and exclusions for each deliverable.
- **b** ❌ Requirements documentation lists what stakeholders need. It feeds the scope statement but does not set out deliverables with their acceptance and exclusions.
- **c** ❌ The scope management plan describes how scope will be defined, validated and controlled. It is a process document, not the description of the scope itself.
- **d** ✅ The project scope statement describes the product scope, deliverables, acceptance criteria and exclusions in detail. It is the document the sponsor is describing.

**The concept.** In predictive delivery, the project scope statement is the detailed description of the project and product scope: deliverables, acceptance criteria and explicit exclusions.

**Why this is correct.** The sponsor asks for three things together: deliverables, how they will be accepted and what is out of scope. That combination is the project scope statement. The charter is tempting because it is also an early document, but it is high level and authorizes the project rather than defining its boundaries in detail.

**How to reason it out**

1. List what the document must contain: deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions.
2. Recall which planning output records all three in detail.
3. Separate it from the plan that describes how scope is managed.
4. Separate it from the charter, which is high level and authorizes the project.

> **Exam tip:** Deliverables + acceptance criteria + exclusions = project scope statement.

</details>

## Question 12

*Process*

**While drafting the business case for a new warehouse management system, the sponsor and operations leads list the expected value: lower inventory carrying cost, fewer picking errors, better staff morale and a stronger reputation for reliable delivery. Which pair of these value components is intangible?** Choose one.

- **a.** Carrying cost and picking errors
- **b.** Staff morale and delivery reputation
- **c.** Carrying cost and delivery reputation
- **d.** Picking errors and staff morale

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: b**

- **a** ❌ Carrying cost is a monetary amount and picking errors are counted directly. Both are tangible.
- **b** ✅ Morale and reputation are real value but have no direct monetary or physical measure, which makes them intangible.
- **c** ❌ Reputation is intangible, but carrying cost is a monetary figure, so the pair is mixed.
- **d** ❌ Staff morale is intangible, but picking errors are a countable operational measure, so the pair is mixed.

**The concept.** Value components are tangible (monetary or directly measurable, such as cost, revenue, error counts) or intangible (goodwill, morale, reputation, brand).

**Why this is correct.** Staff morale and reputation for reliable delivery cannot be expressed directly in money or units, so they are intangible. Carrying cost is monetary and picking errors are counted, so any pair containing either is at least partly tangible.

**How to reason it out**

1. Sort each listed component: can it be expressed in money or counted directly?
2. Carrying cost and error counts can; morale and reputation cannot.
3. Pick the pair where both components are intangible.

> **Exam tip:** Tangible value can be counted or priced; intangible value (morale, reputation, goodwill) still counts but needs indirect measures.

</details>

## Question 14

*Process*

**A predictive project needs a data-migration utility. The team could build it in about four weeks, or a vendor could supply one. Before any bid documents are drafted, the project manager wants to compare the cost, risk and control of each path. Which technique should be used?** Choose one.

- **a.** Source selection analysis
- **b.** Make-or-buy analysis
- **c.** Bidder conference
- **d.** Procurement audit

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: b**

- **a** ❌ Source selection analysis decides how sellers' proposals will be evaluated. It chooses between sellers, not between building and buying.
- **b** ✅ Make-or-buy analysis compares producing the item internally with acquiring it externally, on cost, risk, capability and control. It comes before bid documents because it decides whether there is anything to procure.
- **c** ❌ A bidder conference answers prospective sellers' questions once a procurement is already under way. It assumes the decision to buy has been made.
- **d** ❌ A procurement audit reviews the procurement process at or after closure to capture lessons. It plays no part in the initial decision to buy.

**The concept.** Make-or-buy analysis is the first procurement planning decision: should the work or item be produced in-house or acquired from a seller?

**Why this is correct.** The project manager is comparing two paths, internal build versus vendor supply, before any bidding exists. That is exactly what make-or-buy analysis does, weighing direct and indirect costs, risks, available skills and how much control the organization needs. Source selection analysis is the tempting near-twin, but it chooses among sellers after the decision to buy is made.

**How to reason it out**

1. Note the timing: before bid documents exist.
2. Identify the decision: build internally or acquire externally.
3. Match it to the planning technique that compares those two paths.
4. Rule out techniques that assume a purchase is already happening.

> **Exam tip:** Build or buy comes first; only a 'buy' decision leads to bid documents and seller selection.

</details>

## Question 15

*Process*

**A project manager on a predictive data-center project has rolled up the work package cost estimates and the risk analysis has produced reserves. The sponsor asks what the approved cost baseline will contain. What should the project manager answer?** Choose one.

- **a.** Contingency reserves plus management reserves
- **b.** Work package estimates plus contingency reserves
- **c.** Work package estimates with neither type of reserve
- **d.** Work package estimates plus the management reserve

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: b**

- **a** ❌ Reserves alone are not a baseline; the baseline is built from the estimated cost of the work, with contingency added.
- **b** ✅ The cost baseline is the approved, time-phased sum of the work package estimates and the contingency reserves set for identified risks.
- **c** ❌ Leaving out contingency would make the baseline ignore the identified risks the team has already planned responses and money for.
- **d** ❌ Management reserve is held outside the cost baseline; it is added on top of the baseline to form the overall project budget.

**The concept.** The cost baseline is the approved, time-phased budget used to measure cost performance. It includes contingency reserves but excludes management reserve.

**Why this is correct.** The cost baseline adds the contingency reserves for identified risks (known-unknowns) to the work package estimates. Management reserve covers unidentified work, sits outside the baseline and together with the baseline forms the project budget. The tempting near-twin swaps contingency for management reserve.

**How to reason it out**

1. Start from the work package cost estimates.
2. Add the contingency reserves for identified risks.
3. Leave management reserve out of the baseline.
4. Remember that baseline plus management reserve equals the project budget.

> **Exam tip:** Baseline = estimates + contingency; budget = baseline + management reserve.

## Question 16

*Business Environment*

**A project manager joins a predictive utility project. The sponsor hands over a document that sets who approves budget changes, how often the steering committee reviews progress and which decisions the project manager may take alone. What does this document describe?** Choose one.

- **a.** The communications management plan
- **b.** The project scope statement
- **c.** The project management plan
- **d.** The project governance framework

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: d**

- **a** ❌ The communications plan sets who receives what information, when and how. It does not assign approval authority or decision rights.
- **b** ❌ The project scope statement is part of the scope baseline and describes the deliverables, boundaries and acceptance criteria of the work. Approval authority and steering committee cadence are governance, not scope.
- **c** ❌ The project management plan describes how the work will be executed, monitored and controlled. It operates inside governance; it does not set who holds decision rights above the project manager.
- **d** ✅ Governance defines the oversight structure, decision rights, approval levels and review cadence the project operates within. That is exactly what the document sets out.

**The concept.** Project governance is the framework of structure, rules, decision rights and oversight within which the project is managed.

**Why this is correct.** The document allocates authority (who approves budget changes), sets oversight (steering committee review cadence) and limits the project manager's discretion. Those three elements define governance. The management plan and communications plan are tempting because they also contain procedures, but they describe how the project team works and communicates, not who decides.

**How to reason it out**

1. Look at what the document controls: authority, oversight and decision rights.
2. Ask whether it tells the team how to do the work or who may decide.
3. Decision rights and oversight above the project manager are governance.
4. Plans for executing or communicating the work sit inside that framework.

> **Exam tip:** Governance decides who decides; the management plan decides how the work gets done.

- **d.** Reassign the developers' tasks as the committee member asks

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: a**

**How to reason it out**

2. Note that the committee member is acting outside the agreed decision rights.

**While planning a predictive bridge-replacement project, the project manager lists the obligations the work must meet. <details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: c**

- **d** ❌ Internal policies are set by the performing organization and can be tailored through its own governance. The code is imposed from outside by an authority.

**How to reason it out**

1. Identify who imposes the obligation.
2. Check whether that party can enforce it with legal power.
3. A public authority with enforcement power means a regulatory requirement.

- **d.** Add the module to the scope baseline and plan it

<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: b**

2. Analyse its impact on the baselines and on risk.

## Question 20

*Business Environment*

**During the daily stand-up, a developer on an agile team says the shared test environment has been down since yesterday and three stories cannot be verified. The project manager has not yet looked into the problem. What should the project manager do first?** Choose one.

- **a.** Record the outage as a new entry in the risk register
- **b.** Find out how the outage affects the sprint goal
- **c.** Ask the product owner to remove the three stories
- **d.** Escalate the outage to the infrastructure director

<details>
<summary>Show answer and explanation</summary>

**Answer: b**

- **a** ❌ The outage has already happened, so it is an impediment to remove, not an uncertain future event to analyse in the risk register.
- **b** ✅ Understanding which work is blocked, for how long and whether the sprint goal is threatened tells the project manager how urgent the impediment is and what kind of intervention it needs.
- **c** ❌ Dropping scope is premature. Nobody yet knows whether the environment will be back within hours, so the stories may still be finished this sprint.
- **d** ❌ Escalating before knowing the impact or trying the normal support route skips a step. The outage may be fixed quickly at team or support level.

**The concept.** Evaluating an impediment's impact - which work and goals it blocks, and for how long - comes before choosing how to intervene.

**Why this is correct.** PMI expects the project manager to understand a problem before acting on it. Here the first job is to learn what the outage blocks and whether the sprint goal is at risk; that sets the urgency and the intervention. Escalating, re-scoping or treating the outage as a risk all act before the impact is known, and the last one also misclassifies something that has already happened.

**How to reason it out**

1. Note that the impediment has already occurred and is blocking work.
2. Establish which stories and goals depend on the blocked work.
3. Estimate how long the blockage is likely to last.
4. Choose an intervention that matches the impact.

> **Exam tip:** Assess what an impediment blocks and for how long before deciding how hard to push to remove it.

</details>
